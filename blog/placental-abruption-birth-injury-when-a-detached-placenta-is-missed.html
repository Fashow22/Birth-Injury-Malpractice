Placental Abruption and Birth Injury — When a Detached Placenta Is Missed or the Response Is Too Slow

Legally Reviewed by Nick Reyes, Partner, The Alvarez Law Firm · July 27, 2026

Of all the emergencies that can arise in labor, a placental abruption is one of the hardest to see coming and the least forgiving once it starts. The placenta is the baby’s lifeline — the single organ delivering oxygen from the mother’s blood to the baby’s. When it peels away from the wall of the uterus before the baby is born, that lifeline begins to fail. In a large abruption, the time between a healthy baby and a permanent brain injury can be measured in minutes.

Much of what parents read online focuses on the warning signs a mother might feel — bleeding, a sudden hard cramp, a back that won’t settle. Those signs matter. But the most dangerous abruptions are the ones that don’t announce themselves that way: the concealed abruption with no visible blood, and the abruption whose first and only warning is a change on the fetal monitor. This is a plain-English guide to what a placental abruption is, how it should be recognized and answered, and where the line falls between a sudden event no one could prevent and a preventable injury.

What Is a Placental Abruption, and Why Is It So Dangerous to a Baby?

A placental abruption is the premature separation of the placenta from the uterine wall before delivery. Because the placenta is the baby’s only source of oxygen, any separation eats into the oxygen supply. A small, partial abruption may be tolerated with close monitoring; a large or complete separation can cut off oxygen quickly and cause hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE), cerebral palsy, or death. Abruption is one of the most important obstetric causes of the oxygen deprivation behind catastrophic term brain injury.

The reason abruption is so unforgiving is mechanical: the separated area no longer transfers oxygen at all, and if the separation spreads, the functioning area shrinks with it. In the classic obstetric review by Oyelese and Ananth in the journal Obstetrics & Gynecology, abruption is described as a leading cause of both maternal hemorrhage and fetal oxygen deprivation — which is precisely why a suspected abruption is treated as an emergency measured in minutes, not hours.

Concealed vs. Revealed Abruption — Why the Most Dangerous Kind Has No Bleeding

Not every abruption bleeds where anyone can see it. In a revealed abruption, blood tracks down between the membranes and the uterine wall and escapes through the cervix as vaginal bleeding. In a concealed abruption, the blood is trapped behind the placenta with no easy exit, so there may be little or no visible bleeding even as a large volume is lost behind the placenta. Roughly one in five abruptions is concealed.

This distinction is not a technicality — it is the single most common way an abruption is underestimated. A care team that waits for visible blood before taking a possible abruption seriously can badly misjudge how much placenta has already separated. The amount of blood on the outside says almost nothing about the amount of blood on the inside. The teaching in obstetrics is blunt: the severity of an abruption cannot be judged by the amount of visible bleeding. A mother with a rigid, exquisitely tender uterus and an abnormal fetal tracing may be having a catastrophic concealed abruption while barely bleeding at all.

“She wasn’t bleeding much” is not reassurance — and in the records, it is often the tell. When a chart documents severe abdominal pain, a hard uterus, and a deteriorating fetal heart rate, but the response was slowed because the visible bleeding seemed minor, that reasoning is the opposite of what a concealed abruption calls for. The most severe abruptions are frequently the ones that show the least blood.

Why the Fetal Heart Rate Tracing Is Often the First — and Sometimes Only — Sign

In many abruptions, the earliest warning is not something the mother feels — it is something the fetal monitor shows. As oxygen delivery falls, the baby’s heart rate responds, often before bleeding or pain becomes obvious and sometimes when neither is present at all. The patterns obstetricians associate with an evolving abruption include recurrent late decelerations, a rising baseline, loss of variability, a sinusoidal pattern, and — in a severe abruption — prolonged deceleration or bradycardia that does not recover.

This is why continuous fetal monitoring is so central to catching an abruption in time. The tracing is frequently the first objective evidence that the placenta is failing, and it is recorded automatically, minute by minute, whether or not anyone is watching closely. Our guide to fetal heart rate tracing categories explains why a Category II or Category III tracing is a signal to act, not to keep waiting. In an abruption case, the monitor strip is often the clearest window into exactly when the baby began to decompensate — and how long it took anyone to respond.

How Doctors Tell an Abruption From Other Emergencies

Bleeding late in pregnancy has more than one cause, and the right response depends on telling them apart quickly. The main possibilities a labor team weighs include:

  • Placental abruption — separation of a normally positioned placenta. Classically painful, with a firm, tender uterus; bleeding may be heavy, minimal, or concealed.
  • Placenta previa — a placenta covering or near the cervix. Classically painless bright-red bleeding, usually known in advance from prenatal ultrasound.
  • Uterine rupture — a tear in the uterine wall, a particular risk during a trial of labor after cesarean. Often signaled by an abrupt fetal heart rate change and loss of the contraction pattern. Our guide to uterine rupture during a VBAC covers this emergency in depth.
  • Vasa previa — unprotected fetal blood vessels crossing the cervix, which can bleed fetal blood catastrophically when the membranes break.

The reason the differential matters is that it changes what has to happen next and how fast. A key point that separates careful care from negligent care is that doctors are not supposed to wait for certainty before protecting the baby. When the clinical picture and the fetal tracing point toward an abruption compromising the baby, the standard response is to move toward delivery — not to hold for an ultrasound that may be falsely reassuring or for the bleeding to declare itself.

How Is a Placental Abruption Diagnosed?

Placental abruption is primarily a clinical diagnosis, not an ultrasound diagnosis. This is one of the most consequential and least understood facts about the condition. Fresh blood behind the placenta can look nearly identical to placental tissue on an ultrasound, so a scan frequently shows nothing even when a significant abruption is underway. The obstetric literature has long recognized that ultrasound detects only a minority of abruptions later confirmed at delivery — which means a normal or reassuring ultrasound does not rule out an abruption.

Because imaging is unreliable here, the diagnosis rests on the whole clinical picture: vaginal bleeding (if present), abdominal or back pain, uterine tenderness or a rigid uterus, frequent low-amplitude contractions, and an abnormal fetal heart rate tracing. A Kleihauer-Betke test, which detects fetal blood that has crossed into the mother’s circulation, can support the picture in some cases, particularly after trauma. But no single test confirms or excludes an abruption in the moment. The clinical rule that flows from this — act on the picture, do not wait for imaging — is exactly where many abruption injuries turn.

When Does a Placental Abruption Point to Possible Negligence?

An abruption is not, by itself, evidence of malpractice. Many abruptions are sudden, and some occur in careful hands with a rapid, competent response and a tragic outcome that nothing could have changed. As with every topic on this blog, the legal question is not the event — it is the response. The questions that tend to decide an abruption case are concrete:

  • Were known risk factors — high blood pressure or preeclampsia, prior abruption, abdominal trauma such as a car accident or fall, or cocaine use — recognized, and did they prompt closer monitoring?
  • When the fetal heart rate tracing turned abnormal, was it read as a possible abruption — or explained away as something benign while the baby kept decompensating?
  • Did the team act on the clinical picture, or did it wait for visible bleeding or a confirming ultrasound that an abruption often does not provide?
  • Once an emergency was recognized, was the decision-to-incision time for the cesarean reasonable, with the right people mobilized quickly?
  • Afterward, was the baby promptly evaluated for signs of encephalopathy and, if eligible, for therapeutic cooling within the six-hour window?

When the answers show that clear warnings were missed or that a recognized emergency was answered too slowly — and a baby was seriously injured as a result — the negligence lies in that delayed or disorganized response, not in the abruption itself. When the answers show prompt recognition and a well-run emergency delivery, an abruption reflects a catastrophe of biology rather than a failure of care.

What the Placenta Itself Reveals After Birth

The placenta is physical evidence, and in an abruption case it is often the most objective evidence there is. After any delivery involving bleeding, suspected abruption, or a depressed newborn, ACOG and the AAP recommend that the placenta be sent for pathological examination. A placenta cannot be coached, and it does not depend on anyone’s memory of a chaotic delivery room.

On examination, a pathologist can identify a retroplacental clot — blood that collected behind the placenta — and the compression it leaves on the maternal surface where the placenta was pushed away from the uterine wall. From how organized that clot is, a pathologist can sometimes estimate whether the separation was acute or had been developing longer, which speaks directly to how much time the care team actually had. These are findings no one can argue away years later. It is also why the fate of the placenta matters: when a placenta that should have been examined was instead discarded, that missing evidence can become its own issue in a birth-injury case.

What a Trained Reader Looks For in the Records

Because an abruption unfolds in minutes and leaves its trail across several parts of the chart at once, these cases turn on reading the whole record with both medical and legal training. When Herb Borroto, M.D., J.D., the firm’s Medical-Legal Expert, reviews an abruption file, the pieces are cross-checked against one another rather than read in isolation:

  • The monitor strip against the clock. When did the tracing first turn abnormal, how did it evolve, and how many minutes passed between the first ominous change and the actual delivery?
  • The nursing and physician notes against the strip. Whether what the team documented seeing and doing matches what the monitor was showing at the same moments — and whether “minimal bleeding” was used to justify waiting through a deteriorating tracing.
  • The placental pathology report. The retroplacental clot, its organization, and what it suggests about the timing of the separation — measured against the timeline in the chart.
  • The newborn course. The cord blood gases, the Apgar trend, and whether a baby with signs of brain injury was evaluated for cooling while it could still help.

Alex Alvarez, the firm’s Managing Partner and a Board Certified Civil Trial Lawyer, presents these cases to a jury the way the medicine reads them: a placental abruption is a race against the clock, and the record keeps the time. The monitor, the notes, and the placenta together show when the baby began to fail and how long it took the team to answer — and a timeline built minute by minute from the medical record is far harder to dispute than anyone’s recollection of a frightening night.
